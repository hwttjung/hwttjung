TITLE : Hyundai's Alabama expansion will add hybrid and EREV engine production—but only 53 new jobs

SUMMARY : Hyundai plans to invest nearly $450 million in its Montgomery, Alabama, plant, adding 53 full-time jobs and engine production for hybrid and extended-range electric vehicles.

Content : 
<div contenteditable="false" class="imageBox" style="width: 790px;" title="1725407"> <div class="innerBox"><img id="i1725407" class="imgPhoto" title="Hybrid engine manufacturing " src="https://d.jobsnhire.com/en/full/1725407/hybrid-engine-manufacturing.webp" alt="" width="790"> <div class="captionBox"><span class="credit">Ediotrial Illustration via AI</span></div> </div> </div>
Hyundai Motor Manufacturing Alabama will invest nearly $450 million to expand its Montgomery plant, adding engine production for hybrid and extended-range electric vehicles, according to <a rel="nofollow" href="https://www.madeinalabama.com/2026/10/hyundai-motor-manufacturing-officials-celebrate-major-expansion/" target="_blank" class="external_red">the state-run Made in Alabama site</a>. The governor's office lists 53 new full-time positions, <a rel="nofollow" href="https://constructionreviewonline.com/hyundai-alabama-plant-expansion-targets-hybrid-and-erev-output-in-montgomery/" target="_blank" class="external_red">Construction Review Online reported</a>, and counts capital investment at $412.9 million, a figure below Hyundai's own.

The plant already employs about 4,200 people, and total investment there has reached nearly $2.2 billion since production began in 2005, the state site says. The 53 new jobs would add roughly 1.3% to that workforce, a JobsNHire calculation. The hiring number is modest next to the spending, which is common when a plant adds equipment and automation to existing operations.
<h2>Two Investment Figures, One Project</h2>
The gap between "nearly $450 million" and $412.9 million, about $37 million, is not explained in the sources. It could reflect costs the state does not count as capital investment, a different accounting date, or rounding in the company's description. Without a breakdown, the safest approach is to credit each figure to its source: Hyundai for the larger number and the governor's office for the smaller one. The Construction Review Online report also notes it is unclear whether all <a rel="dofollow" href="https://www.jobsnhire.com/articles/60294/20261029/honeywell-tempe-aerospace-factory-600-jobs.htm" target="_blank" data-sys="1">53 positions</a> are entirely new.

Officials gathered at the plant on Wednesday. "Most importantly, these investments represent confidence," said Mark Rader, the plant's chief operating officer, according to the state site. Governor Kay Ivey also attended, along with state and local leaders, in <a rel="nofollow" href="https://www.einpresswire.com/article/952951859/governor-ivey-joins-hyundai-to-celebrate-major-investment-in-alabama-manufacturing" target="_blank" class="external_red">a celebration the governor's office described</a>. The state says the plant and its suppliers generate more than $5 billion in annual economic impact and about $100 million in state tax revenue each year.
<h2>Which Vehicles the Work Supports</h2>
The expansion adds engine production for hybrid and extended-range electric technologies, known as EREV, and prepares the plant for the refreshed Santa Fe and the all-new Tucson. <a rel="nofollow" href="https://mbiz.heraldcorp.com/article/10918566" target="_blank" class="external_red">The Herald Business</a> links the project to the Tucson hybrid and the Santa Fe EREV. The Alabama Reporter, in a September report, said Hyundai plans to move Tucson hybrid production from South Korea to Montgomery and to launch up to five new models in 2027.

Those details explain why the project is more than an equipment upgrade. Moving production of a vehicle from one country to another shifts work and, over time, jobs. It also ties the plant's performance to how well those vehicles sell. The sources reviewed do not offer sales projections, and they do not say how many of the plant's existing jobs depend on the new models.

For comparison, Hyundai's previous major engine expansion in Montgomery was a $388 million project that <a rel="nofollow" href="https://www.manufacturing.net/home/news/13122966/hyundai-investing-388m-to-update-alabama-plant-build-other" target="_blank" class="external_red">trade press reported at the time as bringing 50 new jobs</a>. The 2026 project is larger in dollars and adds a similar number of positions, which fits a pattern of more automation per job. That is an observation from the figures, not a statement by the company.
<h2>Montgomery Hiring and the Wage Gap in the Data</h2>
For workers in the Montgomery area, 53 positions is a small number but a real one. Plants of this kind often hire in waves tied to equipment installation, and none of the reports reviewed gives a hiring schedule. Job seekers should not expect all 53 positions to open at once, and should check Hyundai's job listings and the state employment service for actual postings.

Wage information is the missing piece. None of the sources reviewed that describe the 53 jobs give an average hourly wage or salary, so readers should not assume a figure. Pay for new roles will be visible in the postings themselves, and benefits, shift premiums, and overtime will matter as much as the base rate.

For the plant's current employees, a new product line can mean training and a chance to move into technical roles. That is an inference, not a company statement, based on the fact that hybrid and extended-range engines use different parts and processes than gasoline-only designs. Hyundai has not described what training is planned or whether existing staff will have first access to the new positions.

The ratio of money to jobs is striking. Using the state's $412.9 million and 53 positions, the project works out to nearly $7.8 million of investment for each new job, a JobsNHire calculation. That points to a capital-heavy project in which machines and tooling make up most of the cost. It also means the expansion matters less as a source of new openings than as a bet on keeping the existing 4,200 jobs tied to vehicles that are in demand. A plant that makes the engines for new models has a stronger case for staying busy than one that does not, although that is a reading of the figures and not a company commitment.

Readers should also keep the scale in mind. Alabama has seen several large auto announcements over the years, and each one has changed local employment only at the margins. The 53 positions will matter to the people who get them and to the suppliers who sell parts to the plant, but they are not a sign that auto hiring is surging across the state or the country.

Three signals will show how the project is going: hiring announcements from the state, Hyundai's job postings for Montgomery, and the start of production for the new models in 2027. Until then, the confirmed facts are the 53 new positions, the 4,200 existing jobs and the two investment figures, each from its own source.

Title : U.S. Employers Added 7.4 Million Jobs and Lost 7.3 Million in First Quarter, BLS Report Shows

Summary : U.S. private employers recorded a net gain of 88,000 jobs in the first quarter of 2026, as gross hiring slowed and job losses increased, BLS data show.

Content:
<div contenteditable="false" class="imageBox" style="width: 790px;" title="1725409"> <div class="innerBox"><img id="i1725409" class="imgPhoto" title="busy American business district" src="https://d.jobsnhire.com/en/full/1725409/busy-american-business-district.webp" alt="" width="790"> <div class="captionBox"><span class="credit">Ediotrial Illustration via AI</span></div> </div> </div>
U.S. private employers added 7.4 million jobs and lost 7.3 million jobs in the first quarter of 2026, leaving a net gain of just 88,000, the Bureau of Labor Statistics said Wednesday in <a rel="nofollow" href="https://www.bls.gov/news.release/cewbd.htm" target="_blank" class="external_red">its Business Employment Dynamics report</a>. The quarter covers December 2025 through March 2026. The net change was 0.0% of private-sector employment once rounded.

The new number is a sharp slowdown from the previous quarter, when BLS counted a net gain of 609,000 jobs. Gross job gains fell by 478,000 from the fourth quarter, while gross job losses rose by 43,000. The report does not say why, and one quarter of data does not establish a trend.
<h2>Millions of Jobs Moving Beneath a Flat Total</h2>
This report is easy to confuse with the monthly jobs report, so the difference matters. The monthly report counts how many jobs there are. The Business Employment Dynamics report counts the movement behind that total: jobs added at businesses that opened or grew, and jobs lost at businesses that closed or shrank. A near-zero net result can hide millions of jobs changing hands.

In the first quarter, gross gains were 5.5% of private-sector employment and gross losses were also 5.5%. Expanding businesses added 5.9 million jobs, and newly opened businesses added 1.5 million. On the other side, shrinking businesses lost 5.8 million jobs, and closing businesses lost another 1.5 million. In plain terms, the economy created and destroyed almost the same number of jobs, and the difference was 88,000.

That churn is normal. <a rel="dofollow" href="https://www.jobsnhire.com/articles/60285/20261029/what-happens-401k-when-you-leave-job.htm" target="_blank" data-sys="1">Workers move, businesses open and shut</a>, and seasonal hiring ends. The more useful question is whether the balance is shifting, and the latest figures say it tilted toward flat. Readers should also note that this data covers the private sector only, and the report includes annual revisions to earlier quarters, so comparisons with older releases should be made with the revised figures.
<h2>Where the Jobs Were Gained and Lost</h2>
By industry, construction posted the largest net gain, at 112,000 jobs. Education and health services added 92,000. Those gains were offset by transportation and warehousing, which lost 162,000 jobs on a net basis, and manufacturing, which lost 30,000. Goods-producing industries as a group gained 96,000, while service-providing industries lost 8,000.

Gross job gains exceeded gross job losses in six of 13 industry sectors, which means most sectors lost more than they added. Geography was more balanced: gains exceeded losses in 29 states and the Virgin Islands. BLS reports those totals but, in the portion reviewed, does not explain the causes. Any link between, for example, warehouse losses and changes in shipping or retail demand would be an interpretation and not a finding of the report.

The report also counted 323,000 new establishments, which accounted for 949,000 jobs. That figure is a reminder that new businesses keep forming even in a slow quarter. Data on closures for the same quarter are not yet available, because BLS says its establishment death data run only through the second quarter of 2025, when 923,000 jobs were lost at 310,000 closing establishments.
<h2>Small Firms Lost Ground, Large Firms Gained</h2>
BLS also reports net job changes by firm size. Firms with 1 to 49 employees had a <a rel="dofollow" href="https://www.jobsnhire.com/articles/60282/20261029/how-profits-layoffs-happen-together.htm" target="_blank" data-sys="1">net decrease of 67,000 jobs</a>, and firms with 50 to 249 employees had a net decrease of 5,000. Firms with 250 or more employees had a net gain of 255,000. These are the published figures, and readers should not add them together and compare the sum with the overall 88,000, because the size groupings and the way BLS handles the data are not fully explained in the portion of the report reviewed.

<a rel="dofollow" href="https://www.jobsnhire.com/articles/60281/20261029/what-do-after-major-layoff.htm" target="_blank" data-sys="1">For small-business owners</a>, the pattern is a warning but not a verdict. A quarter of net losses among the smallest firms is one data point, and BLS does not say whether it reflects closures, layoffs, slower hiring, or something else. Owners deciding whether to hire can look at the figures as context, not instruction.

For workers, the gap between large and small employers may matter in practical ways. <a rel="dofollow" href="https://www.jobsnhire.com/articles/60293/20261029/lenz-therapeutics-workforce-cuts-vizz-marketing.htm" target="_blank" data-sys="1">Larger companies tended to add jobs</a> in this quarter, which may mean more openings in established firms. The report does not say which companies or occupations those are, and it does not measure pay.
<h2>Reading the Numbers Carefully</h2>
Three cautions apply. First, a net gain of 88,000 is small against a private workforce of well over 100 million, and the rounding means the net rate shows as 0.0%. Second, the sharp drop from the prior quarter may be partly due to revisions and seasonal patterns, which BLS adjusts for but cannot fully remove. Third, <a rel="dofollow" href="https://www.jobsnhire.com/articles/60283/20261029/how-tell-if-ai-replacing-workers.htm" target="_blank" data-sys="1">gross figures do not count people</a>. One worker who loses a job and finds another within the quarter appears in both the gain and loss totals.

JobsNHire has reported on other signs of a cautious job market, including a <a rel="nofollow" href="https://www.jobsnhire.com/articles/60066/20260806/us-labor-markets-low-hiring-low-firing-era-hidden-risks-beneath-surface.htm">low-hiring, low-firing labor market</a> and <a rel="nofollow" href="https://www.jobsnhire.com/articles/60101/20260823/us-labor-market-stagnation-forces-shift-hiring-retention-strategies.htm">stagnation that is changing hiring and retention strategies</a>. The new data fit that general picture, but they do not prove it, since they measure a single quarter.

The next report covers the second quarter of 2026 and is scheduled for Jan. 27, 2027, at 10 a.m. Eastern time, according to BLS. That release will show whether the first-quarter slowdown held or whether hiring picked up again. Until then, employers and job seekers should treat the 88,000 as one reading of a slow quarter, not as a forecast. Anyone comparing it with other figures should check that they cover the same period and the same group of workers, since public discussion often mixes monthly payroll counts, job openings, and these quarterly gains and losses as if they were the same thing.

HEADLINE : Honeywell Aerospace Plans New $500 Million Tempe Factory That Is Expected to Create About 600 Jobs

SUMMARY : Honeywell says it plans to build a $500 million aerospace factory in Tempe, Arizona, with about 600 expected jobs and a targeted late 2028 opening.

CONTENT : 

<div contenteditable="false" class="imageBox" style="width: 790px;" title="1725405"> <div class="innerBox"><img id="i1725405" class="imgPhoto" title="Aerospace manufacturing facility" src="https://d.jobsnhire.com/en/full/1725405/aerospace-manufacturing-facility.webp" alt="" width="790"> <div class="captionBox"><span class="credit">Darren Staples | POOL | AFP via Getty Images</span></div> </div> </div>
Honeywell Aerospace plans to build a $500 million, 300,000-square-foot factory in Tempe, Arizona, that it expects to create about 600 jobs, according to <a rel="nofollow" href="https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0002089271/000208927126000031/exhibit992projectarisepres.htm" target="_blank" class="external_red">an investor presentation filed with the Securities and Exchange Commission</a>. The company says the plant is scheduled to open in late 2028, which is two years away.

The announcement is a plan, not a finished project. The presentation describes the investment, the building size, and the job estimate, but the sources reviewed do not report a construction start date, a hiring timeline, or the kinds of jobs that will be posted first. Readers should treat the 600 as an expected figure, not as positions that exist or are open today. Factory projects of this size often change between announcement and opening, and the company's own timeline is the only schedule available.
<h2>A Factory Built Around Automation</h2>
The presentation says the plant would use digital production methods, modular robotics, automated inspection, and digital twins, which are computer models of a physical product or process. It would also add complex machining. In plain terms, Honeywell is describing a factory where <a rel="dofollow" href="https://www.jobsnhire.com/articles/60283/20261029/how-tell-if-ai-replacing-workers.htm" target="_blank" data-sys="1">machines and software do more</a> of the repetitive and measuring work, and where people run, program, and maintain them.

That has a direct bearing on the kinds of jobs. A plant with heavy automation tends to need technicians, machinists, quality inspectors, maintenance staff and engineers, not large numbers of assembly workers. The presentation does not break down the 600 jobs by type, so the exact mix is unknown. Anyone considering whether to train for the roles should watch for Honeywell's job postings and for announcements from Arizona economic development agencies instead of guessing.

The company named products it expects the site to support, including Assure actuation systems and Attune cooling products, as well as work tied to a $15 billion book of Control Systems wins. Those are company claims in a document for investors, and they have not been independently verified by outside analysts in the material reviewed.
<h2>Honeywell's Earlier Arizona Plans and the $500 Million Question</h2>
The presentation links the Tempe plan to a March agreement between Honeywell and the Department of War, which the company has described as a framework to increase production. <a rel="nofollow" href="https://www.kold.com/2026/03/29/honeywell-aerospace-agrees-500-million-deal-increase-production" target="_blank" class="external_red">KOLD reported</a> on that agreement in March, and it noted that Honeywell has more than 7,000 employees in Arizona. Both announcements mention $500 million, and both involve Honeywell Aerospace in Arizona, which is why the question comes up. It is not clear from the sources whether they describe the same money or two separate commitments, so readers should not add them together.

Honeywell Aerospace trades as HONA and has more than 36,000 employees around the world, according to the presentation. Against that base, 600 new jobs would add roughly 1.7% to its global staff, a JobsNHire estimate based on those two figures. Within Arizona, where it already employs thousands, the addition would be about 8% of the stated 7,000 base, though that is also an estimate and the two numbers come from different sources.

JobsNHire has reported before on how Honeywell's corporate restructuring affects staffing, in its story on <a rel="nofollow" href="https://www.jobsnhire.com/articles/60231/20261023/honeywell-technologies-ends-first-standalone-quarter-promising-eliminate-stranded-costs-year-end.htm">Honeywell Technologies' first standalone quarter</a>, where headcount plans were left unstated. The new Tempe plan is separate, and it is one of the few places where the company has put a number on hiring. It also fits a wider pattern of aerospace and defense suppliers adding factory capacity as demand for their products stays high. For example, <a rel="nofollow" href="https://www.jobsnhire.com/articles/60230/20261023/lockheed-martin-backlog-reaches-record-292-billion-f-35-deliveries-fall-its-release-silent.htm">Lockheed Martin's record backlog</a> shows how much work is lined up for the industry, although that release said nothing about hiring.
<h2>Preparing for Jobs That Are Two Years Away</h2>
The plant is two years from opening, so the sensible steps are modest. Workers in the Phoenix area who want these jobs can look at community college manufacturing and robotics programs, which often train for the technician roles that automated plants need. They can also watch <a rel="dofollow" href="https://www.jobsnhire.com/articles/60284/20261029/ai-hiring-tools-applicant-rights-guide.htm" target="_blank" data-sys="1">Honeywell's careers site</a> and state workforce announcements. The sources reviewed do not describe any hiring program or training partnership tied to the Tempe plant.

For current Honeywell employees, the question is whether any of the 600 jobs will be filled from within. The presentation does not say. That is common at the announcement stage, and the answer usually becomes clear only when the company posts openings.

There are also open questions about the cost and the economics. Large factory announcements often involve state or local incentives, tax treatment or utility agreements. The sources reviewed do not mention any incentive package for the Tempe site, so none should be assumed. If one is approved, public records from Tempe or the state of Arizona would show its terms, including any job targets the company has to meet.

The wider labor picture adds a note of caution. A single plant does not change a regional or national trend. As a data point, the Bureau of Labor Statistics reported this week that manufacturing had a small net loss of jobs in the first quarter, a figure covered in a separate JobsNHire story today. A 600-job plant is meaningful for the Tempe area, but it is one project, not a sign that factory hiring is broadly rebounding.

The next milestones are practical ones. Look for a groundbreaking date, a public hearing or permit filing in Tempe, and the <a rel="dofollow" href="https://www.jobsnhire.com/articles/60274/20261028/job-scam-warning-signs-how-protect-yourself.htm" target="_blank" data-sys="1">first job postings</a>. Until then, the confirmed facts are the $500 million investment, the 300,000-square-foot building, the roughly 600 expected jobs and the late 2028 opening target, all stated by Honeywell itself.
